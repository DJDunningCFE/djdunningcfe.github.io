How I work

Fraud operations is where I honed the method, and the method itself reaches well beyond fraud. A case that goes wrong is usually a symptom: somewhere between what a process was designed to achieve and what it actually produces, there is friction — a step that depends on someone remembering, a control that is available but optional, a handoff with no clear owner.

Finding that friction is the investigative half, and it has to be right — nothing downstream works if the diagnosis is wrong. But an investigation that ends in a documented finding leaves the same failure available tomorrow, so the work continues into the second half: changing the system so the friction is gone rather than recorded. Procedures matter, and I have authored my share of them — they tell people what they should do. Systems decide what people can do, and a change made there holds without depending on anyone remembering.

The second half is where most of the value sits. Changing a live process is deliberate work — it takes review, approvals, and the time of people whose own work the change touches, and it should. All of that is already spent by the time the original gap closes, so the question I ask next is what else this same change can carry: fewer steps, fewer judgment calls, less rework, a more accurate result. The problem that started it often ends up being the smallest thing the change accomplished.

Built

Fraud Ambassador Program — eight branches

Designed and lead an executive-approved program placing a trained fraud point of contact in every branch. Built the training standards, documentation expectations, and escalation path from nothing, and trained nine ambassadors plus the Manager of Branch Administration and training staff. Now in its second annual cycle.

Friction
Frontline staff who spotted something wrong had no fast route to fraud expertise. Guidance moved through slow, passive channels that people had to think to go looking for.
Change
A named person in every branch with a direct line to the fraud team, so the path from noticing to escalating is one step instead of several.
Result
Ambassadors independently stopped losses in three separate instances within the program's first month — and questions that used to travel to the fraud team now resolve at the branch.

Turning an optional control into a structural one

Casework surfaced a pattern where a verification step was reaching its intended outcome only some of the time — not because it failed when used, but because using it was a judgment call. I documented the gap, made the case for changing the requirement itself rather than issuing guidance about it, and authored the revisions that followed.

Friction
A control that depended on someone deciding to invoke it, on every individual occasion.
Change
The control became a required part of the process rather than an available option, organization-wide — so the gap closed by default instead of by diligence.
Also
Removing the judgment call took a decision out of the workflow entirely. The step became consistent across every case and every person, which made it quicker to complete and far easier to audit.

Measuring a control so the change could be argued

A control threshold was being debated from individual cases, which makes every loss look like an exception rather than a pattern. I built the reporting structure that tracks it — deciding which measures actually matter, including derived ones that separate recovered loss from unrecoverable, and loss prevented from loss taken — so the case put to senior leadership rests on the shape of the data instead of on the last bad week.

You will not find figures anywhere on this page. The numbers belong to my employer and stay there. What travels is the method — deciding what to measure, and why that measure answers the question being asked.

Friction
No standing measure of what the threshold was costing, so the argument restarted from zero every time it came up.
Change
A defined set of measures, captured as events occur, that show the trend and let the genuine exceptions be seen as exceptions.
Also
The same measures show which detection routes are actually stopping these, not just what was lost — which is a different and more useful question than the one that started it.
