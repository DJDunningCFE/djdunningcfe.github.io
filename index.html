How I work

Fraud investigation is where I learned the method, but the method itself is not really about fraud. A case that goes wrong is usually a symptom: somewhere between what a process was designed to achieve and what it actually produces, there is friction — a step that depends on someone remembering, a control that is available but optional, a handoff with no clear owner.

Finding that friction is the investigative part. The part I care about is what comes after: changing the system so the friction is gone rather than documented, and so the same failure isn't available a second time. Procedures describe what people should do. Systems determine what they can do, and that is the level worth working at.

The second half is where most of the value sits. Getting approval to change a live process is slow, so closing the original gap is a poor return on that cost by itself. Once the system is open, the question worth asking is what else this same change can do — fewer steps, fewer judgment calls, less rework, a more accurate result. The problem that started it often ends up being the smallest thing the change accomplished.

Built

Fraud Ambassador Program — eight branches

Designed and lead an executive-approved program placing a trained fraud point of contact in every branch. Built the training standards, documentation expectations, and escalation path from nothing, and trained nine ambassadors plus the Manager of Branch Administration and training staff. Now in its second annual cycle.

Friction
Frontline staff who spotted something wrong had no fast route to fraud expertise. Guidance moved through slow, passive channels that people had to think to go looking for.
Change
A named person in every branch with a direct line to the fraud team, so the path from noticing to escalating is one step instead of several.
Result
Ambassadors independently stopped losses in three separate instances within the program's first month — and questions that used to travel to the fraud team now resolve at the branch.

Turning an optional control into a structural one

Casework surfaced a pattern where a verification step was reaching its intended outcome only some of the time — not because it failed when used, but because using it was a judgment call. I documented the gap, made the case for changing the requirement itself rather than issuing guidance about it, and authored the revisions that followed.

Friction
A control that depended on someone deciding to invoke it, on every individual occasion.
Change
The control became a required part of the process rather than an available option, organization-wide — so the gap closed by default instead of by diligence.
Also
Removing the judgment call took a decision out of the workflow entirely. The step became consistent across every case and every person, which made it quicker to complete and far easier to audit.
